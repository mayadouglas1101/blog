WHAT HAPPENS WHEN THE WORLD ENDS

CATEGORY: MISCELLANEOUS

Fair warning: this one's corny. Sincerely, unironically corny. You have to agree to that before you keep going.

Whether the most important part of incident response is 'lessons learned' is apparently up to CompTIA, since unbacked claims are apparently fair game in standardized tests. Hey, nothing wrong with being authoritative. So here's another immutable rule of the universe. Everyone lives through experiences that they couldn't fathom were possibilities. A personal black swan event? Such as a decades long employee losing their job. An education comes to a halt during a pandemic. Something that is such a given that you don't even register it as a variable until it's lost. In 2024 I temporarily worked at a liquor store while waiting on a job across the other side of the country. It seemed pretty set in stone all things considered - and Microsoft was doing layoffs - so I figured 'hey, might as well get a chill job while the other one comes through. What are they going to do. Say no?' They said no. Not even for personal reasons, just a good old 'the position is no longer available.' I couldn't even close out of the email app fast enough because my hands were numb from being in the fridge all day, stocking craft beer. In retail. With a computer science degree. I went from someone in between two prestigious jobs to a blue collar worker from a white collar background. The world ended. So how do you survive an apocalypse? For one, every disaster gives you rubble. That's something to work with. I had Microsoft on my resume at 23. Six months, contract. Still counts. Now every apocalypse movie has a sequel that's much worse than the original. The liquor store I had an out for - I was waiting for a job. A tiny IT shop? A little harder to defend. Sure, it was an ego thing. It was also a security function that was piles of unfinished audits, tooling no sane person could defend, and one actual priority: uptime. I'd taken classes on non-deterministic Turing machines. I was doing desktop support. The worst part was that I am, and always will be, awful at IT. And that was the biggest clue. 1. I'm terrible at IT. I don't care about ports. There are plenty of people who love memorizing ports. And some of us have a personality (not to be confused with infosec or security operations - I like to think I'm pretty good at that). 2. Apparently business acumen is bad for your career because it's hard to sit through indefensible business decisions when your paycheck depends on it. 3. I studied for Security+ and eJPT between shifts at a liquor store. I read Brian Krebs for fun. Nobody asked me to do either. I eventually built projects, blogs, and labs. Not because it was career oriented, but because it had absolutely nothing to do with uptime. The word 'romantic' is mostly associated with relationships at this point which is kind of sad in the transactional way. The useless shit surrounding digital crime of all things was exactly the point. Across an Arby's parking lot, I built an alter ego. A hacker name. Cult of the Dead Cow might largely be inactive but their legacy isn't. My hacker name was Ace of Hearts. Unlike NewportMentholLover69420 (original), it had meaning. I don't play cards. Not a fan. But the suits were sitting right there. Security is played in spades. Adversary, kill chain, red team, blue team, blast radius - every word we have for ourselves is a war word. The commercial layer is diamonds: retainers, uptime, the invoice that clears whether or not anybody got safer. Nobody plays hearts. So commit to the bit. Not the 'put in the hours' part since that's not really a high bar. But be present, engaged, and ready to take the fall, even if it makes you look dumb. Along with a salary of 63k, my MSP also had decommissioned computer parts, which gave me an idea. My very own OSINT research environment. I VLAN segmented, spun up a VPN, used a clean Linux image, and hardened my PC. And got fucking owned. Not only did I get malware on my computer, but I wasn't even able to fully analyze what the hell went wrong. Never found out how. No lessons learned. I did everything right and got owned anyway. So when someone tells me the user is the weakest link, I know what they're describing. They're describing me. The crash course on operating systems was fascinating since the stakes were higher than any college lecture I've taken. There is no checklist that makes you immune to risk. Not all the good decision making in the world changes the fact that a lot of life is simply up to chance. What matters is that you consider the odds before making a decision. I eventually started applying to jobs and A/B testing my resumes. Not because online applications are reliable or the market is stellar, but because surviving an apocalypse comes down to statistics. And I iterated. And I iterated again. And continued building on the only material I had - rubble. While I'd love to end this in a 'Making six figures at an awesome sexy defense AI cyber startup (I do)' moral of the story, I don't think that this is quite the end. None of this was ever permanent. Not then, not now. I wish I could tell you I'm harder to own these days. Excuse me while I go find out where this process came from.
