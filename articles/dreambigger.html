DREAM BIGGER

CATEGORY: TECH + SOCIETY

We're all doing women in cybersecurity a disservice when we tell them to just dream bigger. It feels good to say and it's encouraging to hear. But then careers stagnate. Salaries stay below market rates. Work goes from creation to brute labor. Dreams just stay dreams but they never really materialize. And here's my take. It's kind of .. self-induced? That sounds like a psychotic take since the subset of women who go into traditionally 'male-coded fields' tend to be more ambitious to begin with. But that doesn't change the fact that you're socialized into some 'factory settings' that might be counterproductive to said ambition. A coworker told me recently that most people learn their moral frameworks from sitcoms. Sounds reductive. A lot clicked for me there though, because ethics is simultaneously under-analyzed as a formal discipline and incredibly effective at social conditioning. We absorb what gets rewarded, what gets punished, what the good woman looks like by the end of the episode. And the good woman is humble. Patient. Grateful. She doesn't ask for too much. You've probably met the coworker who makes you say, "wait, shouldn't you be making twice as much? Somewhere else?" Not a nepo hire accusation. Just a genuine, slightly baffled observation of the discrepancy between value and pricing. That person's framework didn't get the same update. They skipped the episode. I've run into this at almost every tech job I've been at - but there was a notable example that kind of embodied this phenomenon for me. She had the work ethic. She was putting in the hours, doing the work, genuinely talented. I'm not talking 'know what a laptop is.' She could be doing novel research at Anthropic, yesterday, if she wanted to. And the ambition was there. It just got pointed at effort instead of leverage. She was working hard as a strategy for being noticed rather than going and getting what she was worth. And the environment rewarded that. Called it dedication. Called it a strong work ethic. Never called it what it was, which was a talented person doing service work for service pay because nothing in her framework told her she was allowed to want more. She wasn't getting certified either. Not because she couldn't. Because somewhere along the way she'd absorbed the idea that wanting more credentials, more recognition, more options, was asking for too much. Better to keep her head down and hope the right person noticed. Nobody noticed enough for her to get out of a 60k service job. My mom's Japanese which means I'm familiar with the dynamics of 'Notice Me Senpai' when I see it. The thing about the children of immigrants is that they have an insane amount of agency. After all, they did see their parents teleport to a new world and start from scratch. Now the immigrant kid jokes of waaaay over-indexing on prestige credentials exist for a reason. Asians are the only non-billionaires that use 'state school' as an insult. However - it's ingrained in us for a reason. Portable vs Internal credentials. What makes you legible enough to succeed in another country? The universal language of 'oh, this person is probably qualified for (insert anything.)' This could be something as simple as a degree or a certification. In cybersecurity that would be something like a Security+. I've procrastinated on a driver's license for almost 10 years (and counting) so I understand test anxiety, but do yourself a favor and at least get one piece of paper, no matter how humiliating it is to relive middle school and shuffle flashcards for a test. Even something like a portfolio, research, or a talk will be visible and tied to your name even if you get canned by a budget cut. Cool. Now you're in the room. When we say, 'Dream Bigger', we don't mean get to a cubicle and warm a chair. That's where internal credentials come in. And here's where the sitcom ethics come back. A lot of women don't take initiative because the framework they absorbed says you wait to be asked. You don't volunteer, you don't overstep, you don't build something nobody assigned you. That's not humility, that's the episode where the ambitious woman learns her lesson. Taking initiative gets coded as pushy. Doing work outside your scope gets framed as not knowing your place. So you wait. And the guy next to you who absorbed a completely different set of lessons just builds the thing and gets promoted off it. That guy is not your enemy. He's actually your proof of concept. He didn't wait for permission. He didn't wonder if it was his place. He just built the thing. He'll even encourage you to do it - people who care about the craft, care about the craft. Not your gender. It worked for him. Which means it's negotiable. This is what visible women in the field actually demonstrate. Not that it's easy, not that the penalties aren't real, but that the math is wrong. Anna Pham was publishing research and building a public presence before she had the institutional backing to justify it by conventional logic. Jen Easterly ran CISA, one of the most visible and consequential roles in federal cybersecurity, and was loud about it, specific about it, unambiguous about her expertise and her authority. Neither of them waited for the episode where they earned the right to be seen. That's what role models actually provide. Not inspiration. Counterevidence. Proof that someone looked at the same environment, the same field, the same set of unwritten rules, and got different results by operating from a different framework. The guy in the other room who just builds things isn't doing something you can't do. He's just running different factory settings. Let's do your reset.
